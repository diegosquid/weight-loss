**GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with an increased risk of gallbladder and biliary disease in randomized trials.** That does not mean most people develop gallstones, or that a single pooled estimate predicts your personal risk. The useful questions are how large the effect was, which patients were studied, and which symptoms deserve attention.

## What the trial evidence shows

A 2022 meta-analysis combined 76 randomized trials involving 103,371 participants. Its search covered studies available through mid-2021; it is not a comparison of every product or dose available today. [Original study: He and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35344001/).

| Outcome | Relative risk, with 95% confidence interval |
|---|---|
| Gallbladder or biliary disease, combined | 1.37 (1.23–1.52) |
| Gallstones, also called cholelithiasis | 1.27 (1.10–1.47) |
| Gallbladder inflammation, or cholecystitis | 1.36 (1.14–1.62) |
| Biliary disease | 1.55 (1.08–2.22) |

The combined association was larger in weight-loss trials: RR 2.29, compared with 1.27 in trials for type 2 diabetes or other diseases. Higher-dose and longer-duration trial groups also showed stronger associations. These are subgroup findings, not instructions to change a prescription. [Study results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35344001/).

### A 37% relative increase is not a 37% chance

Relative risk compares the event rate in one group with another. An RR of 1.37 means the measured rate was 1.37 times the comparator rate. It does **not** mean 37 of every 100 treated people developed the outcome.

As a purely mathematical illustration, a baseline risk of 1% multiplied by 1.37 becomes 1.37%, an increase of 0.37 percentage points. That example is not an observed trial rate or an estimate for an individual. Always look for the baseline rate and follow-up period when comparing treatment risks.

## Why weight loss complicates the picture

Rapid weight loss can itself increase gallstone risk. NIDDK explains that changes in cholesterol released into bile and gallbladder emptying can contribute, particularly during very rapid loss or prolonged periods without eating. That background makes it inappropriate to attribute every gallstone solely to a medication. [NIDDK: dieting and gallstones](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gallstones/dieting).

The meta-analysis supports an association; it does not tell us precisely how much each proposed mechanism contributes in one person. Nor does it establish that a particular diet, supplement or slower dose increase prevents medication-associated gallbladder events.

For tirzepatide, consult the product's own warning and trial data rather than assigning it the older pooled class estimate. Our [Mounjaro dosage explainer](/medications/mounjaro-dosage-chart/) separates label information from automatic escalation advice.

## Symptoms that need prompt assessment

Seek medical care promptly for abdominal pain lasting several hours, particularly with vomiting, fever or chills, yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, or pale stools. NIDDK lists these as possible signs of complications involving the gallbladder, liver or pancreas. [Symptoms and causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gallstones/symptoms-causes).

Do not use an online symptom list to distinguish a gallbladder attack from pancreatitis or another cause of severe abdominal pain. Familiar nausea after an injection does not make a new, persistent or severe symptom harmless.

Delayed stomach emptying is a different issue. Our [GLP-1 and gastroparesis explainer](/glp-1/glp1-gastroparesis-stomach-paralysis/) discusses that distinction; it cannot diagnose the cause of pain.

## What to discuss with the prescribing team

Bring a history of gallstones, previous gallbladder surgery, recent weight changes and a list of medicines. Useful questions include:

- Does my history change the balance of benefit and risk?
- Which symptoms should trigger same-day or emergency assessment?
- Who should decide what happens to the next dose if symptoms occur?
- What follow-up would be needed if testing finds gallstones?

There is no universal stop-and-restart schedule for suspected gallbladder disease. If treatment must change, [weight-regain research](/glp-1/weight-regain-after-glp1/) is useful background for a follow-up conversation, not a reason to delay urgent assessment.

Gallbladder disease is also distinct from fatty liver disease. See our [GLP-1 and fatty liver overview](/glp-1/glp1-fatty-liver-masld/) for that separate topic.
