**There is no clearly established “best supplement” for lasting weight loss.** Evidence varies by ingredient and formulation, and a small average change in a study does not make a branded product an effective treatment. Start by separating three questions: does the ingredient help, does this product match the evidence, and is it appropriate for you?

## Weight-loss supplements: a comparison of the evidence

| Ingredient or product type | What the evidence supports | Main limitation or concern |
|---|---|---|
| Green tea extract / caffeine | At most modest effects on weight in some studies | Stimulant effects; concentrated green tea extract has been linked to liver injury |
| Berberine | Uncertain weight benefit; a 2026 trial found no advantage over placebo | Results depend on the population; digestive effects and drug interactions |
| Glucomannan | Little or no weight-loss effect in the NIH evidence summary | Gastrointestinal effects; obstruction concerns with some tablet products |
| CLA | Small effects on weight or body fat | Benefits may be too small to matter; gastrointestinal and metabolic concerns |
| Protein powder | A way to add dietary protein | Its label is not proof of a weight-loss effect; assess the full diet and product |

Ingredient summaries draw on the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements review](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/WeightLoss-HealthProfessional/) and [NCCIH's berberine assessment](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/berberine-and-weight-loss-what-you-need-to-know). This is not a ranking of tested brands.

## Why “clinically studied” may not describe the product

A study can evaluate a single ingredient while the product on sale contains a blend. The amounts, formulation and participants may differ. NIH highlights these problems when interpreting weight-loss supplement research. [NIH evidence review](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/WeightLoss-HealthProfessional/).

Consider a seller who points to a trial without showing the amount of the ingredient in its own formula. You cannot check whether the two match. Even a matching amount would leave other questions: what was the comparator, how long did the study run, and did the improvement persist after the study ended?

A testimonial provides a different kind of information from a controlled trial. It does not tell you what would have happened without the supplement, what else changed, or how representative the result was.

## Does berberine help with weight loss?

NCCIH concludes that weight-loss evidence is not conclusive. Many studies have high risk of bias, and results vary across formulations and populations. Berberine can cause digestive symptoms and interact with medicines, including cyclosporine. NCCIH also raises concerns for infants, pregnancy and breastfeeding. [NCCIH assessment](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/berberine-and-weight-loss-what-you-need-to-know).

A [randomized trial published in January 2026](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2844037) adds a useful reality check. It enrolled 337 adults in China with obesity and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), but without diabetes. At the studied dose of 1 gram per day over six months, berberine did not outperform placebo on the primary measures of visceral fat area and liver fat. Body weight, a secondary outcome, also did not differ significantly between groups. Both groups received lifestyle guidance. This is a study description, not a dosing recommendation.

This study does not settle every question about berberine, other formulations or people with diabetes. It does show why a seller should not turn preliminary findings into a promise to reduce belly fat. Bring the exact ingredient label to a pharmacist or clinician instead of choosing a dose from a marketing comparison.

## Green tea extract, fiber and CLA: avoid a blanket safety claim

NIH's review describes small or uncertain weight effects for these ingredients and meaningful differences in adverse effects. “Natural” does not establish safety. Green tea as a beverage and a concentrated extract should not be treated as interchangeable exposures. [NIH review](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/WeightLoss-HealthProfessional/).

“Fiber supplement” is also a broad category. A result for psyllium cannot simply be assigned to glucomannan, inulin or a mixed product. The same caution applies when one study measures body fat and a seller describes the finding as a large change in scale weight. Always check what was actually measured.

## What do FDA status and third-party testing actually tell you?

In the United States, FDA does not approve dietary supplements for safety and effectiveness before they go on sale. The manufacturer is responsible for safety and labeling; this differs from the approval process for medicines. A claim that a supplement is “FDA approved” should therefore prompt a closer look. [FDA 101: Dietary Supplements](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/fda-101-dietary-supplements).

Independent quality testing can help establish whether a product contains its listed ingredients and meets specified contaminant limits. It does not establish that the product causes weight loss or is appropriate for your health conditions. NIH describes this distinction in its [guide to supplement quality](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/WYNTK-Consumer/).

For a practical comparison, imagine two bottles: one offers a verifiable quality seal, and the other offers only testimonials. The first may provide better information about manufacturing quality. You still need a separate answer to the efficacy question. Neither a seal nor a satisfied customer's story supplies a controlled weight-loss trial.

## Check for hidden ingredients and unsupported promises

The FDA maintains notifications about weight-loss products found to contain dangerous hidden ingredients. The agency cautions that it cannot identify every contaminated product; absence from a warning list is not evidence of safety. [FDA weight-loss product notifications](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud-notifications/weight-loss-product-notifications).

Before buying, use this checklist:

1. **Identity:** is the exact product and manufacturer clear?
2. **Amounts:** are individual ingredient quantities disclosed?
3. **Evidence:** was the finished formula studied, or only an ingredient?
4. **Outcome:** is the claim about weight, body fat, appetite or a laboratory marker?
5. **Safety:** has a qualified professional checked the formula against your medicines and conditions?
6. **Cost:** what is the full monthly cost, including subscriptions and additional products?
7. **Alternative:** what useful action could you take without buying it?

## Can you combine supplements with weight-loss medicine?

Do not assume an over-the-counter supplement is compatible with a prescription simply because both are marketed for weight management. FDA notes that supplement ingredients can conflict with medicines or existing conditions. A clinician or pharmacist needs the actual formula, not just the words “fat burner” or “natural.” [FDA supplement guidance](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/dietary-supplements).

Bring the complete label, serving size, other supplements and your medication list to that discussion. Include caffeinated drinks when reviewing a stimulant-containing product. This article cannot clear a specific combination, and a supplement should not be used as a substitute for prescribed treatment. If a suspected adverse reaction occurs, stop taking the supplement and seek medical advice, as [FDA recommends](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/fda-101-dietary-supplements).

## If the real problem is planning meals

A supplement assessment may reveal that the immediate need is practical: deciding what to eat, shopping consistently or having meals ready. Start by identifying that need. Buying a different category of product should not be presented as a treatment for the same medical condition.
