**Start budget meal prep with the portions you expect to eat, then check what you must spend and where those portions will be stored.** A large batch is only useful if it fits your schedule, appetite and storage space.

You do not need special containers, a subscription or a new cookbook to try this. Use food-safe containers you already own and one familiar recipe. The examples below demonstrate planning and cost arithmetic; they are not tested recipes, current grocery prices or a nutritionally complete menu.

## Choose a batch you can finish

Write down the meals you actually need before deciding how much to cook. Four lunches for one adult could require four portions. Two adults eating those lunches would require eight, with quantities adapted to each person's needs. A recipe's stated serving is a starting point for shopping, not a rule about how much everyone should eat.

Choose a familiar plant-based dish with a protein food, such as beans, lentils or tofu, alongside vegetables and a starchy food you enjoy. Keep its full recipe and cooking instructions. For ideas on choosing meals, our [beginner plant-based planning guide](/metabolism/plant-based-meal-planning-beginners/) starts with two small batches.

Check whether you will still want the same dish on the last planned day. If not, prepare less, or choose two recipes that share an ingredient. Our [grocery-list guide](/metabolism/weekly-grocery-list-from-recipes/) explains how to combine their quantities without mixing up dry, cooked and drained weights.

## Separate the checkout bill from the food used

There are two different budget questions:

- **Can I afford this shopping trip?** Count the full packages you need to buy, including ingredients absent from your pantry.
- **What does this batch use?** Count the share of each ingredient that goes into the food you prepare, including supplies bought earlier.

Here is a **fictional accounting example in US dollars** for ingredients allocated to four portions of a bean-and-vegetable pasta dish. The prices and allocations are invented to show the method. They omit ingredient quantities and cooking directions, so this table cannot be used as a recipe. Assume everything listed must be purchased, with no tax or delivery charge.

| Ingredient group | Packages bought today | Value used in this batch | Value remaining for later |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Pasta | $4.00 | $1.00 | $3.00 |
| Ready-cooked canned beans | $3.00 | $3.00 | $0.00 |
| Vegetables | $4.00 | $2.00 | $2.00 |
| Canned tomatoes | $2.00 | $2.00 | $0.00 |
| Oil and seasonings | $5.00 | $1.00 | $4.00 |
| **Total** | **$18.00** | **$9.00** | **$9.00** |

The checkout requires **$18**, even though the batch uses **$9** of ingredients. Dividing $9 by four gives **$2.25 in ingredients per planned portion**. Dividing the entire $18 bill by four would also charge those meals for ingredients still available for later use.

For your own calculation, multiply a package's price by the fraction used, using consistent measures. If you use one quarter of a $4 pasta package, record $1. Count the cost of any bread, sauce or other food served alongside the dish. Cooking energy, transport, delivery, containers and your time are separate costs; this example does not estimate them.

### Recalculate when food goes uneaten

If one of the four equal portions is discarded and only three are eaten, the same $9 batch has cost **$3 per portion actually eaten**. The lost portion's ingredients still cost money. Do not count a portion kept safely in the freezer as waste simply because you have not eaten it yet.

The remaining $9 of ingredients in the table is useful inventory, not cash returned to your wallet. Give it a place in a later recipe. If some is discarded, include that loss when reviewing your wider grocery spending instead of continuing to call it available stock.

## Compare usable food, time and storage space

Use local shelf prices and compare equivalent quantities. A large package with a lower price per ounce may still exceed today's cash budget. For canned foods, check whether the recipe uses the liquid or a drained amount before comparing yields.

You can compare fresh vegetables with plain frozen or canned options that suit the recipe. Similarly, compare ready-cooked beans with dried beans while accounting for preparation time and the cooking instructions each requires. Do not assume either format is always cheaper in your shop, or substitute dry and cooked quantities one for one.

The [EPA's household food-waste guidance](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/preventing-wasted-food-home) recommends checking existing supplies, planning quantities and buying bulk offers only when you can use the food before it spoils. Put a use for the remainder beside a purchase before calling the larger pack a saving.

## Assign fridge and freezer portions before cooking

For a four-portion batch cooked on Sunday, this is one possible **storage plan**, not a full week's eating plan:

| Portion | Planned meal | Storage decision on Sunday |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Monday lunch | Refrigerate promptly |
| 2 | Tuesday lunch | Refrigerate promptly |
| 3 | Wednesday lunch | Freeze promptly for later use |
| 4 | Friday lunch | Freeze promptly for later use |

Label containers with the dish, cooking date and intended meal. If you have no suitable freezer space, make a smaller batch and cook again later. Keep toppings separate when the recipe calls for fresh assembly.

[USDA's leftover-safety guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/leftovers-and-food-safety) recommends:

- Refrigerating perishable leftovers within **two hours**, or **one hour above 90°F / 32°C**. Use shallow containers for rapid cooling; do not leave a large pot on the counter until completely cold.
- Keeping the refrigerator at **40°F / 4°C or below** and using refrigerated leftovers within **three to four days**, or freezing them for longer storage.
- Thawing safely, for example in the refrigerator, and reheating leftovers to **165°F / 74°C**, checked with a food thermometer. When microwaving, cover, stir or rotate, and check several places.

Follow food-specific and package instructions where these are stricter. In this example, move each frozen portion to the refrigerator in time to thaw before its planned meal. Discard food left out beyond safe limits; a savings target is not a reason to keep it.

## Keep nutrition separate from the cost calculation

A low ingredient cost does not establish that a meal, or the whole day's food, meets your needs. A few plant-based lunches also do not require you to follow a fully vegan diet.

For a fully vegan diet, the [NHS explains the need for varied foods and reliable sources of nutrients including vitamin B12, calcium and iodine](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/how-to-eat-a-balanced-diet/the-vegan-diet/). Fortified foods and supplements may be needed. This guide does not assess individual requirements; seek qualified dietary advice when a medical condition or other specific need affects your food choices.

## Try the free method before paying for more recipes

Save one recipe, write its portions and ingredients, record the package costs, and assign a storage destination to every portion. After using the batch, note what you ate, what you saved and what you discarded. Adjust the next batch using those observations.

The [NHS Healthier Families recipe library](https://www.nhs.uk/healthier-families/recipes/) offers free recipes and dietary filters. Check each ingredient list: the library includes animal foods, and vegetarian does not necessarily mean vegan.
