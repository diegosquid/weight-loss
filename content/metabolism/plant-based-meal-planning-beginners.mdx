Start plant-based meal planning with **two meals you would willingly eat twice**. Check which ingredients you already have, put the meals on specific days and buy only what is missing. You can practice this with four dinners before trying to organize an entire week of breakfasts, lunches and snacks.

Below is a worked shopping example for one adult. It is a planning exercise with simple meal ideas, not a tested recipe collection or a nutritionally complete daily menu. Adjust quantities and ingredients to your appetite, household and dietary needs.

## Choose the meals before making the shopping list

First, count the evenings when you expect to cook at home. Include travel, late work shifts and meals out. A plan that requires cooking on a night you are unavailable is already difficult to follow.

For each dinner, choose a plant protein such as beans, lentils or tofu; a starchy food such as pasta, potatoes or bread; and vegetables you enjoy. Then decide how you will season it. This is a way to organize ingredients, not a formula that guarantees adequate nutrition.

Here, “plant-based” means practicing meals built around plant foods. You do not have to change everything else you eat to try them. A fully vegan diet excludes animal foods and requires attention to the overall diet, including fortified foods and nutrients discussed below.

## A small first plan: four dinners, two cooking sessions

This example uses two batches, each divided into two dinner portions. Cook the pasta meal on Monday and the bean filling on Wednesday; the following evening uses the refrigerated second portion. Change the days to suit your week.

- **Monday — chickpea, tomato and spinach pasta.** Make two portions; refrigerate the second promptly.
- **Tuesday — the second portion of pasta.** Reheat the stored portion safely.
- **Wednesday — black bean, pepper and spinach wraps.** Make filling for two dinners; refrigerate half separately from the tortillas.
- **Thursday — wraps with the remaining filling.** Reheat the filling and assemble fresh wraps.

For the pasta meal, cook the pasta according to its package. Combine drained canned chickpeas, canned tomatoes and half the spinach in a saucepan, and cook until the vegetables are cooked through. Season and combine with the pasta. For the wraps, cook sliced peppers with the remaining spinach, then add drained canned black beans and cumin. Divide the filling between the two dinners, with two tortillas and some salsa for each.

These are suggested combinations rather than kitchen-tested recipes; use a recipe with detailed cooking instructions if you are unfamiliar with the method. Keep the ingredient quantities from that recipe when making your own list.

## Grocery list for this four-dinner example

The amounts below cover **both batches together**, not each meal separately. Can weights are the labeled contents before draining; package sizes vary. If you already have an ingredient, subtract it before shopping.

### Canned foods

- **Chickpeas:** 1 can, about 15 oz / 425 g, for both pasta portions.
- **Black beans:** 1 can, about 15 oz / 425 g, for both wrap dinners.
- **Chopped tomatoes:** 1 can, about 14–15 oz / 400–425 g, for the pasta sauce.

### Pasta and bread

- **Dry whole-wheat pasta:** 6 oz / 170 g for both pasta portions.
- **Medium tortillas:** 4, allowing 2 for each wrap dinner.

### Vegetables

- **Frozen chopped spinach:** 10 oz / 280 g, half in each batch. Cook according to the package.
- **Bell peppers:** 2, for both wrap dinners.

### Condiments and pantry ingredients

- **Salsa:** ¼ cup / 4 tablespoons, half for each wrap dinner.
- **Olive oil:** about 2 tablespoons in total for cooking the two batches.
- **Ground cumin:** 1 teaspoon for the beans; black pepper to taste as preferred.

### Account for package sizes

You may need to buy a larger package than the amount used. For example, a 16 oz pasta box leaves 10 oz dry pasta after this plan. Write that remainder into your pantry list for next time. Leave unused frozen spinach frozen, and follow the salsa and tortilla labels for storage after opening.

For two adults eating these same four dinners, double the listed ingredient quantities. That gives eight dinner portions. This is arithmetic for shopping, not a recommendation that everyone should eat the same portion size.

## Make substitutions that keep the plan manageable

Use kidney beans in the wraps if those are already in your cupboard, or choose a pasta shape you like. If replacing canned beans with dried beans, follow a suitable recipe and its cooking directions; they are not interchangeable by can weight or cooking time.

Check labels when avoiding animal ingredients or managing allergies. Pasta and tortillas can contain ingredients you need to avoid. A vegetarian label alone does not mean vegan, and plant-based does not mean allergen-free.

If one meal appeals to you and the other does not, replace the second before shopping. Reusing an ingredient only helps if you want to eat the result. Keep a simple backup you already know how to prepare for a night when your plans change.

## Plan storage as well as cooking

Leftovers need prompt refrigeration. USDA guidance says to refrigerate them at 40°F / 4°C or below within two hours, or within one hour when the surrounding temperature exceeds 90°F / 32°C. It generally recommends using refrigerated leftovers within three to four days. The example above uses each second portion the next day. See USDA's guidance on [handling leftovers safely](https://ask.fsis.usda.gov/article/How-do-I-handle-leftovers-safely) and [refrigerated storage time](https://ask.fsis.usda.gov/article/How-long-can-you-keep-leftovers-in-the-refrigerator).

Use shallow containers for cooling and follow the food's storage and reheating instructions. If your schedule changes, freeze a suitable portion promptly rather than keeping the whole week's cooked food in the refrigerator. Foods stored unsafely cannot be made safe simply by reheating them.

## A dinner list does not cover a whole vegan diet

Breakfast, lunch, snacks and drinks still need their own plan. The NHS describes a balanced vegan diet as including varied plant foods, fortified foods and supplements where needed. Vitamin B12 deserves particular attention: reliable vegan sources include foods fortified with B12, and a supplement may be needed. Do not assume that ordinary beans, vegetables or an unfortified plant drink provide it.

Other nutrients also need consideration, including calcium, iodine, vitamin D and iron. Read the [NHS guide to the vegan diet](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/how-to-eat-a-balanced-diet/the-vegan-diet/) for the broader picture. If you need a diet adapted for a medical condition, pregnancy or a child's growth, seek advice from a qualified dietitian or clinician rather than treating this dinner example as a full eating plan.

## Start with free recipes; consider a cookbook only if it solves a problem

Our comparison of [free recipes and paid meal plans](/metabolism/free-recipes-vs-paid-meal-plans/) helps you decide whether you need more meal ideas, a shopping-list tool or help organizing the week before spending money.

The [NHS Healthier Families recipe library](https://www.nhs.uk/healthier-families/recipes/) offers free ideas with meal, preparation-time and dietary filters. It includes animal foods as well as vegetarian dishes, so inspect each ingredient list if you want a vegan meal. Save two recipes, check their serving counts and make a list from those quantities.

For another example of how a shopping list can be grouped by grocery section, see the [US Department of Veterans Affairs plant-based grocery list](https://www.nutrition.va.gov/NUTRITION/docs/UpdatedPatientEd/PlantBasedDietTheGroceryListDec2024.pdf). It accompanies a different, larger menu; its quantities do not belong to the four-dinner example above.

After trying the meals, note what you enjoyed, what remained unused and whether the cooking days worked. Use those three observations to adjust your next list before adding more recipes.
