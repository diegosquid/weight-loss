**Build your grocery list from the quantities in your chosen recipes, adjusted for the servings you intend to cook.** Add matching ingredients together, subtract usable supplies at home, then translate what is missing into packages you can buy.

You can do this in a notebook or a phone note. The worked example below shows the arithmetic, including an easy-to-miss difference between the amount a recipe needs and the amount a package contains. It is a shopping exercise, not a complete menu or a personalized diet.

## 1. Decide what the list needs to cover

Count the meals you will prepare before your next shopping trip. Include breakfasts, lunches, snacks and drinks if this is meant to be your whole grocery list. Mark meals out and planned leftovers so you do not buy ingredients for a second batch you will not cook.

The [EPA's guidance on preventing food waste at home](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/preventing-wasted-food-home) recommends planning around meals you enjoy, checking existing supplies and putting quantities on the shopping list. Start with familiar recipes that fit your available cooking time.

Keep each recipe's link or page number beside its name. You need its ingredient amounts and stated yield; a dish name alone is not enough to calculate the shopping.

## 2. Adjust each recipe before adding ingredients together

Use this calculation for ingredient quantities:

**Amount in the recipe × planned servings ÷ original servings = amount for your batch.**

If a recipe makes four servings and you want two, multiply its ingredients by ½. If it makes two and you want four, multiply by 2. Planned servings include portions you intend to save, not just those eaten immediately.

This changes shopping quantities. It does not mean cooking time, pan size or every part of the cooking method scales in the same way. Follow a recipe's directions for changing batch size, especially for baking.

### Worked example: two recipe batches

These are **invented ingredient excerpts for demonstrating the calculation**, not tested recipes. Sauces, seasoning and other ingredients are omitted here; include every ingredient when using real recipes.

- **Recipe A, a pasta dish:** the original four servings use 200 g dry pasta, 240 g drained chickpeas and 200 g spinach. For two servings, record **100 g dry pasta, 120 g drained chickpeas and 100 g spinach**.
- **Recipe B, a chickpea dish:** the original two servings use 240 g drained chickpeas and 150 g prepared bell pepper. For four servings, record **480 g drained chickpeas and 300 g prepared bell pepper**.

The two batches provide six recipe servings in total. They do not represent six nutritionally equivalent meals or a full week's food. For two people, one possible schedule is Recipe A once and Recipe B divided between two dinners, with appropriate storage.

## 3. Combine only ingredients measured in the same form

The chickpea entries above match: both are weights **after draining**. Add 120 g and 480 g to get 600 g. The consolidated quantities for this excerpt are:

- Drained chickpeas: **600 g**.
- Dry pasta: **100 g**.
- Spinach: **100 g**.
- Prepared bell pepper: **300 g**.

Keep the ingredient's form in its name. “100 g dry pasta” and “100 g cooked pasta” are not interchangeable entries. Neither are dried chickpeas and drained canned chickpeas. Use the recipe's specified form or an appropriate conversion for that food; do not add the numbers as though they mean the same thing.

Also keep weight and volume separate until you have a valid conversion. A cup of chopped spinach and a cup of chickpeas do not weigh the same. If two recipes use different measures, use a weight supplied by the recipe or measure the ingredient; do not invent a universal cups-to-grams conversion.

## 4. Subtract what is already available

For each ingredient, subtract only food that is usable, suitable for the recipe and not already allocated to another meal. Your fridge, freezer and pantry are all part of the check.

Suppose you have one unopened can labeled as providing **240 g drained chickpeas**, 60 g dry pasta and 100 g prepared bell pepper, but no spinach. The missing amounts are:

- Chickpeas: 600 − 240 = **360 g drained**.
- Pasta: 100 − 60 = **40 g dry**.
- Spinach: 100 − 0 = **100 g**.
- Bell pepper: 300 − 100 = **200 g prepared**.

If you have more than needed, write “buy none” and keep the remainder in your inventory. Do not create a negative shopping quantity. If an ingredient is reserved for lunch, it cannot also be counted as available for dinner.

## 5. Convert missing amounts into purchases

You need 360 g drained chickpeas, but the example cans each provide 240 g drained. One can is too little; two provide 480 g. After using the missing 360 g, **120 g drained chickpeas remain** from the new cans.

That calculation uses an explicitly stated drained yield, not the total weight of beans plus liquid. Package sizes and labeling vary. Check the actual product rather than assuming every can provides the same usable amount.

For the pasta, a hypothetical 250 g bag covers the missing 40 g and leaves **210 g dry pasta** for another plan. You buy the bag, but only 40 g belongs to this example's recipe requirement. This distinction matters when comparing a recipe's ingredient cost with the amount you must pay at the checkout.

Your shopping note could now read:

- **Canned foods:** two chickpea cans, each with 240 g drained yield.
- **Pasta:** a package supplying at least 40 g dry pasta; the example uses a 250 g bag.
- **Vegetables:** 100 g spinach and enough bell pepper to provide 200 g after removing the stem and seeds.
- **Still to add:** the actual recipes' remaining ingredients and food for other meals.

Do not add the missing ingredient amount and the purchase amount as two separate items. “360 g chickpeas” and “two cans” describe the same need at different stages of the calculation.

## 6. Put a date beside anything you plan to save

A weekly shopping list is not a reason to refrigerate every cooked portion for seven days. [USDA's leftover guidance](https://ask.fsis.usda.gov/article/How-do-I-handle-leftovers-safely) says to refrigerate at 40°F / 4°C or below promptly, within two hours, or one hour above 90°F / 32°C. Use refrigerated leftovers within three to four days or freeze them for longer storage.

For the example, decide when you will use the second chickpea-dish dinner and the 120 g remaining drained chickpeas. Follow the food's storage instructions after opening; choose a later recipe or freeze suitable food promptly if you will not use it in time. Do not count food that has been stored unsafely as available inventory.

## A final check before shopping

Read the list back against the recipes: correct number of batches, matching units, every ingredient included, household supplies deducted once and packages large enough for what is missing. If someone else will shop, keep the required form and quantity visible—“chickpeas” alone loses the useful detail.
